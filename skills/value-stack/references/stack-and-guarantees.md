# Building the stack, guarantees, mechanism and scarcity

Based on Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers* (2021), read through public summaries (Shortform, linked in the README
credits). Where summaries disagree, this file says so instead of picking one.

## Problems, then solutions, then trim

1. Walk the buyer's path: before they start, while you deliver, after the result. At each step list what goes wrong
   or what they fear ("I have to give an outsider access", "it will drift again next month").
2. Turn each problem into a solution item. Write it as what the buyer gets, not as your task.
3. Pick the delivery form per item: done for them, done with them, or done by them (a template or a guide). Done for
   them lowers effort the most and costs you the most.
4. Trim: score each item on value to the buyer and cost to you. Keep high value with low cost. Drop low value. High
   value with high cost becomes a higher tier or a separate offer.
5. Check that every objection on the list is killed by an item, the guarantee or the proof.

## The guarantee

Every guarantee needs four parts:

| Part | Example |
|---|---|
| Name | Shelf-Match Guarantee |
| Number (what result) | a spot check of 50 SKUs matches the shelf within 1 unit |
| Window (how long) | within 7 days of the first count |
| Consequence (on a miss) | we redo the month free, and if it still misses you pay nothing |

Common types, as summaries of the book list them:

- **Unconditional:** money back, no questions, inside a window.
- **Conditional:** money back (or more) if the buyer did their part and the result did not come.
- **Anti-guarantee:** no refunds, said openly, with the reason (for example, the work cannot be returned).
- **Implied:** pay on results (a share of savings, pay per outcome).

Summaries do not agree on the exact list and names. Some list four types; some add "service" guarantees (we keep
working until it is done) as their own type. Treat these as options, not a fixed taxonomy, and pick by what the buyer
fears and what you can afford.

## The mechanism

The named method behind the result. It does two jobs: it raises likelihood ("there is a method, not a promise") and
it makes the offer hard to compare item by item. Test: could a rival put the same words on their page? If yes, it
is a feature, not a mechanism. Name it after what it does ("3-pass count match"), not with a trademark-style word
that explains nothing.

## Scarcity and urgency, honestly

Real reasons: delivery capacity ("two people, each count takes a full day"), a cohort with a start date, a seasonal
deadline the buyer already has, a price that rises on a stated date for new buyers. Write the reason next to the
claim. No reason means no claim.

## Naming the offer

A short name that says the result or the timeframe ("The Shelf-Match Month") beats a clever one. The name is used in
the page title, the guarantee and the sales call.
